## Slide 2 — Short run and long run

This caveat comes before the lecture models. Short run and long run identify different adjustment assumptions, not universal durations. In the sticky-price short-run demand experiments, firms can vary production and policy can change real financing conditions when expected inflation does not adjust one-for-one. In the standard flexible-price long-run benchmark, output equals its current potential and real saving/investment fundamentals determine r*. A purely nominal shock then mainly changes nominal prices, inflation and nominal rates. That monetary-neutrality claim is a model benchmark, not a theorem about every persistent policy change. Productivity, capital, labour, preferences and fiscal institutions can also change, moving potential output and the real benchmark themselves. Shocks can leave lasting scars, adjustment need not be monotone, and market asset prices can react immediately to expected long-run effects. Never transfer a curve-shift result between horizons without checking assumptions. Define the output gap as 100(Y−Y*)/Y* orally if needed; Lecture 1 introduced it, and the later policy section revisits it. Ask: which prices, quantities and expectations are allowed to move in this experiment?

## Slide 9 — A real return measures purchasing power

If one unit of money grows to 1+i, while the relevant price basket rises by the factor 1+pi, the purchasing-power factor is their ratio. This exact ex post equation defines the realised real return. It does not set the nominal rate, inflation or the real rate independently. All rates refer to the same horizon and price basket. The next pages distinguish a logarithmic/first-order approximation from the long-run Fisher effect.

Reference: [KOM, 12th Global Edition, ch. 16, printed pp. 459–462, 480–482](https://drive.google.com/file/d/1-UaatD4D8-K1UynWK7AkRZ2fAZ3y04mI/view). PDF page = printed page + 1 in the supplied copy.

## Slide 10 — The Fisher approximation comes from logarithms

For this algebra, r and pi are the realised matched-period quantities from the preceding page. Taking logarithms of the exact purchasing-power conversion is exact. Replacing each log(1+x) by x is first-order Taylor approximation around zero. With 5% nominal return and 3% inflation, the exact realised simple real return is 1.05/1.03−1=1.94175%, versus the approximate 2%. The product term omitted by the approximation matters more at high rates. Continuously compounded rates satisfy an additive relation exactly.

Reference: [KOM, 12th Global Edition, ch. 16, printed pp. 459–462, 480–482](https://drive.google.com/file/d/1-UaatD4D8-K1UynWK7AkRZ2fAZ3y04mI/view). PDF page = printed page + 1 in the supplied copy.

## Slide 11 — For decisions today, expected inflation matters

A decision today depends on expected purchasing power, so the forward-looking Fisher approximation uses expected future inflation. Ex ante and ex post real returns differ when inflation surprises. Under uncertainty, E[(1+i)/(1+pi)] is not generally (1+i)/(1+E pi); do not label the expected-inflation ratio an exact identity. Risk premia and the price index also matter when comparing actual assets. The expected real rate is approximated here by i−pi^e, with matched horizons. The Fisher effect is a further economic comparative-static claim, developed after the real-rate equilibrium diagrams.

Reference: [KOM, 12th Global Edition, ch. 16, printed pp. 459–462, 480–482](https://drive.google.com/file/d/1-UaatD4D8-K1UynWK7AkRZ2fAZ3y04mI/view). PDF page = printed page + 1 in the supplied copy.

## Slide 13 — Saving, investment and the real rate

This is a closed-economy, full-employment saving/investment model, not the ex post S=I identity alone and not the short-run IS curve. Desired saving rises with r; desired investment falls. The invented functions, with r in percent, are S=10+3r and I=40−3r. Their intersection gives r=5 and S=I=25. At r=3, households want to save 19 while firms want to invest 31; the real financing price must rise in this model. Real resources, preferences and technology determine the equilibrium benchmark r*. Bank creation of deposits does not create additional real machines or labour. The model does not assume banks merely lend out a fixed pool of existing deposits.

## Slide 14 — A larger harvest can lower the real rate

Suppose a better harvest raises resources today, and households want to save part of the extra income for the future. At the old 5% rate, saving plans rise from 25 to 35 while investment demand remains 25. In the assumed model, savers accept a lower real return; lower rates reduce desired saving and make more investments worthwhile. New saving supply is S=20+3r. Solve 20+3r=40−3r to obtain r=3.3333 and S=I=30. This is a specified outward saving shift, not a universal statement that every productivity shock lowers rates: investment opportunities and expected future resources may shift too.

Reference: [KOM, 12th Global Edition, ch. 19, printed pp. 647–648](https://drive.google.com/file/d/1-UaatD4D8-K1UynWK7AkRZ2fAZ3y04mI/view). PDF page = printed page + 1 in the supplied copy.

## Slide 15 — Better projects can raise the real rate

Keep saving behaviour fixed. More productive projects increase desired investment at a given real rate, from I=40−3r to I=50−3r. At 5%, investment demand is now 35 while desired saving is 25. Financing becomes more expensive until some marginal projects drop out and households save more. The intersection is r=6.6667, S=I=30. Technology, endowments and tastes shape r* through these decisions; nature is a shorthand for these real fundamentals, not a literal institution setting a market price.

## Slide 16 — The long-run Fisher effect

This original diagram illustrates the textbook Fisher effect, not just the purchasing-power conversion. ## Slide 17 — The causal story depends on the horizon

Long-run real fundamentals and the monetary regime jointly determine the nominal/real/inflation configuration. A credible nominal framework helps anchor expected inflation; the real equilibrium and Fisher relation then constrain the nominal rate. In a short-run policy experiment with expected inflation initially fixed, a policy hike raises the real rate and reduces demand. A long-run Fisher correlation therefore does not imply that raising the policy rate mechanically causes higher inflation. Neither direction of causality follows from algebra alone. The later policy rule is a possible response to shocks, not the identity.

Reference: [KOM, 12th Global Edition, ch. 16, printed pp. 459–462, 480–482](https://drive.google.com/file/d/1-UaatD4D8-K1UynWK7AkRZ2fAZ3y04mI/view). PDF page = printed page + 1 in the supplied copy. ## Slide 18 — Inflation measures a change in the price level

P denotes the aggregate price level throughout this lecture. It differs from the lower-case bond price p_0 used in the ZCB example. Inflation is a percentage change in P over a stated interval. A higher price level does not require every buyer to purchase exactly the same real quantities or nominal money supply to rise mechanically. Changes in output, nominal spending and the use of existing balances can all matter.

## Slide 19 — Prices can rise without any extra money

The toy economy has one final consumption good. Money spent is held at 100 over the period; available output falls from 10 to 5. Market-clearing price doubles from 10 to 20. Buyers obtain fewer goods, rather than finding another 100 to buy the original quantities. This illustrates a supply-induced price-level increase, not an estimate of any inflation episode. In a many-good economy, one product becoming expensive may be a relative-price change rather than the same proportional increase in the aggregate price level.

## Slide 20 — New money and faster spending are other possibilities

MV=PY is an accounting relation when V is defined as nominal GDP divided by the chosen monetary aggregate. V is income velocity, not a count of every financial or intermediate-goods transaction. Existing balances can fund different final purchases over a period, while a new bank loan can create a deposit as shown later. Higher prices may reflect more nominal spending, lower real output or both. The identity does not say V is fixed or prove that any particular money-growth statistic caused inflation. Fiscal transfers may change who holds balances or create deposits depending on the financing and banking operations; they do not automatically create new real goods.

## Slide 33 — Real money balances measure purchasing power

Deflating M by P measures purchasing power in units of the representative basket. With unchanged nominal money and doubled prices, the real balance halves. Real money balances are a stock, while output Y and transactions occur over a period; M/P is not itself GDP. Higher real activity typically requires more transaction liquidity at a given rate, which is why Y enters money demand. ## Slide 34 — The money-market condition

This is a conditional equilibrium between real money supply and private desired balances. Interest is the opportunity cost of holding non-interest-bearing transaction money relative to bonds. Modern deposits can pay interest; in that case the relevant opportunity cost is the spread between alternative assets and money, so the classic specification is a simplification. The model chooses a fixed money aggregate and fixed P; market i clears it. In the later rate-target model, the monetary quantity adjusts to implement bar i.

## Slide 51 — Agents adjust portfolios, and bond prices move

## Slide 83 — A two-date endowment economy

In this pure exchange example there is one representative consumer, no production/investment, and a one-period real bond. Aggregate consumption must equal aggregate endowment at each date. Bond holdings sum to zero, but an equilibrium price still determines the rate at which households would want to shift consumption. This is an optional exact foundation for the prior verbal statement.

## Slide 84 — The individual intertemporal budget

Value both dates in current consumption units. Future consumption and future endowment are discounted at the same real bond rate. Maximising the log utility from the previous slide subject to this budget gives the Euler equation on the next. Aggregate market clearing then selects the rate that makes the individual plan equal available endowments.

## Slide 85 — Market clearing pins down a real rate

The Euler equation compares the marginal utility cost of saving one unit now with the discounted marginal utility gain later. With log utility and market clearing, substitute the endowments. More future resources raise the rate; more patience, a larger beta, lowers it. This special equilibrium is not a universal formula for an observed market rate.
