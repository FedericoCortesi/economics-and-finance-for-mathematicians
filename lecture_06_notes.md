## Slide 4 — A firm borrows dollars but earns home currency

The equity loss is 30, or 60% of initial equity. This intentionally simple example isolates a currency mismatch. Dollar export revenues or a hedge could offset it; domestic asset values may also change. Greater exports do not automatically outweigh a sudden solvency and funding problem.

## Slide 5 — Trade and financial channels can oppose each other

The trade channel can improve competitiveness under the relevant pricing assumptions. The financial channel can reduce net worth, tighten collateral constraints and force deleveraging. A country’s net external position alone may hide a vulnerable private sector with large gross foreign-currency liabilities.

Reference: [Sergeyev, Bocconi 30054, lecture 16, PDF pp. 27–33](https://drive.google.com/file/d/1sTSlr7jtSTYf6p7J9-vUSX9OVYPUNh-a/view?usp=drivesdk) [BIS, Financial and trade channels of exchange rates](https://www.bis.org/publications/qr-201612/does-financial-channel-exchange-rates-offset-trade-channel). ## Slide 6 — Debt and equity share bad states differently

This is a payoff distinction, not a claim that all equity financing is safe or preferred. Debt may be indexed, contingent or restructured, and equity owners can demand high returns or sell abruptly. The textbook’s alternative-forms-of-finance explanation is useful here because it connects current-account financing to state-contingent risk sharing.

## Slide 8 — A first-generation crisis mechanism

In the classic model the central-bank balance sheet has domestic credit and foreign reserves backing monetary liabilities. With fixed money demand at the peg, credit expansion requires reserve loss. The conflict is between policy paths. The currency does not become safe merely because reserves remain positive today.

Reference: [Sergeyev, Bocconi 30054, lecture 15, PDF pp. 12–29](https://drive.google.com/file/d/1rgf3G50zalEf92mIjVDxEZNZeYGuuEyf/view?usp=drivesdk) [KOM, 12th Global Edition, ch. 18, printed pp. 557–560](https://drive.google.com/file/d/1-UaatD4D8-K1UynWK7AkRZ2fAZ3y04mI/view). ## Slide 9 — Reserves can be exhausted by an anticipated attack

Forward-looking investors can accelerate the reserve loss. In a fully specified first-generation model, an attack occurs when the post-peg shadow exchange rate meets the peg, not necessarily when a naïve linear extrapolation first hits zero. The jump date here is illustrative and is not calibrated from an unstated money-demand model.

Reference: [Sergeyev, Bocconi 30054, lecture 15, PDF pp. 18–29](https://drive.google.com/file/d/1rgf3G50zalEf92mIjVDxEZNZeYGuuEyf/view?usp=drivesdk) [KOM, 12th Global Edition, ch. 18, 20–22, printed pp. 557–560, 661–668, 705–726, 746–760](https://drive.google.com/file/d/1-UaatD4D8-K1UynWK7AkRZ2fAZ3y04mI/view). ## Slide 10 — A second-generation crisis mechanism

This class of models allows the government’s optimal policy to depend on market expectations. A peg may survive when investors trust it and fail when they coordinate on an attack. Fundamentals still determine whether multiple outcomes are possible; expectations cannot overturn arbitrary levels of fiscal and reserve strength.

Reference: [Sergeyev, Bocconi 30054, lecture 16, PDF pp. 9–23](https://drive.google.com/file/d/1sTSlr7jtSTYf6p7J9-vUSX9OVYPUNh-a/view?usp=drivesdk) [KOM, 12th Global Edition, ch. 18, 20–22, printed pp. 557–560, 661–668, 705–726, 746–760](https://drive.google.com/file/d/1-UaatD4D8-K1UynWK7AkRZ2fAZ3y04mI/view). ## Slide 11 — A coordination example

This original toy example uses an exogenous defence-capacity threshold and abstracts from a government optimisation problem. It illustrates coordination, not a quantitative currency-attack model. A fee makes a failed attack costly. The payoff matrix next is an assumed net payoff in a common numeraire.

## Slide 12 — The coordination game

Each ordered pair is the net payoff to investor 1 and investor 2. Given the other stays, exiting loses one and staying gives zero. Given the other exits, exiting gives two and staying gives zero. With very high defence capacity, both exits would fail; with very low capacity, a single exit could succeed. This explains why fundamentals affect the equilibrium set.

Reference: [Sergeyev, Bocconi 30054, lecture 16, PDF pp. 16–23](https://drive.google.com/file/d/1sTSlr7jtSTYf6p7J9-vUSX9OVYPUNh-a/view?usp=drivesdk) [KOM, 12th Global Edition, ch. 18, 20–22, printed pp. 557–560, 661–668, 705–726, 746–760](https://drive.google.com/file/d/1-UaatD4D8-K1UynWK7AkRZ2fAZ3y04mI/view). ## Slide 13 — A backstop can change incentives

Distinguish coordination failure from a genuinely insufficient expected fiscal resource base. Liquidity assistance can stabilise the good equilibrium when claims are solvent under normal funding, but cannot ensure every promised real payment is feasible. The same distinction recurs in bank runs and sovereign refinancing.

Reference: [Sergeyev, Bocconi 30054, lecture 16, PDF pp. 16–23](https://drive.google.com/file/d/1sTSlr7jtSTYf6p7J9-vUSX9OVYPUNh-a/view?usp=drivesdk) [KOM, 12th Global Edition, ch. 20, printed pp. 661–668](https://drive.google.com/file/d/1-UaatD4D8-K1UynWK7AkRZ2fAZ3y04mI/view). ## Slide 15 — The benefits and costs of a common currency

Optimum currency area theory weighs monetary efficiency benefits against lost stabilisation flexibility. Labour mobility, wage/price flexibility, fiscal risk sharing, shock symmetry and financial institutions affect the trade-off. Membership also changes political and legal constraints, which a simple chart cannot summarise.

Reference: [Sergeyev, Bocconi 30054, lecture 19, PDF pp. 3–15](https://drive.google.com/file/d/1VXG47YMen5K_lOCpHiAywqvmzpbPl7-g/view?usp=drivesdk) [KOM, 12th Global Edition, ch. 21, printed pp. 705–715](https://drive.google.com/file/d/1-UaatD4D8-K1UynWK7AkRZ2fAZ3y04mI/view). ## Slide 16 — An integration trade-off

As integration rises, transaction-cost benefits may rise and the cost of giving up exchange-rate adjustment may fall. The crossing is conditional on the drawn functions. More asymmetric shocks shift costs up; better fiscal insurance or labour mobility can shift costs down. Do not use this invented integration index as an empirical joining rule.

Reference: [Sergeyev, Bocconi 30054, lecture 19, PDF pp. 5–15](https://drive.google.com/file/d/1VXG47YMen5K_lOCpHiAywqvmzpbPl7-g/view?usp=drivesdk) [KOM, 12th Global Edition, ch. 21, printed pp. 705–710](https://drive.google.com/file/d/1-UaatD4D8-K1UynWK7AkRZ2fAZ3y04mI/view). ## Slide 17 — A common currency does not make every price identical

Say euro area, rather than Europe: several European economies use other currencies. A common ECB policy does not equalise national consumption weights or all local prices. Differences can reflect baskets, energy contracts, fiscal measures and services. These are mechanisms, not claims about any country today.

Reference: [ECB, Inflation differentials](https://www.ecb.europa.eu/press/economic-bulletin/focus/2024/html/ecb.ebbox202405_05~6924b2283a.en.html). ## Slide 18 — Different baskets can produce different inflation

Exact one-period fixed-base calculation: A is 0.10×20+0.90×2=3.8%; B is 0.30×20+0.70×2=7.4%. These are invented countries, not actual euro-area observations. National inflation measures also differ because component price changes themselves can differ. Actual HICP calculation is more detailed; this isolates the weights mechanism.

## Slide 19 — A shared nominal rate can mean different real rates

These are expected inflation rates over the loan horizon, not the realised basket inflation on the previous page. The same nominal loan rate is an illustrative assumption; actual household and corporate spreads differ across borrowers and countries. The ECB reacts to area-wide conditions, so a common stance can interact with differing local conditions.

## Slide 20 — Euro government bonds can also have different yields

Sovereign yields can differ with expected default/recovery risk, liquidity and other market premia despite common currency. National fiscal prospects matter through these channels. Avoid asserting an exact additive decomposition or inferring spreads solely from national inflation. The ECB source documents the conceptual credit/liquidity distinction; no historical spread is presented as current.

Reference: [KOM, 12th Global Edition, ch. 21, printed pp. 716–725](https://drive.google.com/file/d/1-UaatD4D8-K1UynWK7AkRZ2fAZ3y04mI/view). PDF page = printed page + 1 in the supplied copy.

## Slide 21 — Relative prices can adjust without a currency change

Use q=E Q_foreign/Q_home from Lecture 4. With the nominal quote fixed, persistent higher home inflation lowers q. That can reduce relative price competitiveness under the relevant trade assumptions. Productivity and equilibrium real-price changes matter too, so not every national inflation gap is a policy failure.

Reference: [KOM, 12th Global Edition, ch. 16, printed pp. 472–481](https://drive.google.com/file/d/1-UaatD4D8-K1UynWK7AkRZ2fAZ3y04mI/view). PDF page = printed page + 1 in the supplied copy. ## Slide 23 — Banks transform maturities

Separate solvency, the value of assets relative to liabilities, from liquidity, the ability to pay when due. Deposits need not all be held in cash. Deposit insurance, lender-of-last-resort facilities and prudential requirements affect run incentives but also create design and moral-hazard questions.

Reference: [Sergeyev, Bocconi 30054, lecture 20, PDF pp. 16–22](https://drive.google.com/file/d/1CU1mi1ebsvbc5dh_VNRv7eoDWNOGfdwE/view?usp=drivesdk) [KOM, 12th Global Edition, ch. 20, printed pp. 661–668](https://drive.google.com/file/d/1-UaatD4D8-K1UynWK7AkRZ2fAZ3y04mI/view). ## Slide 24 — A sovereign refinancing feedback

A stock of long fixed-rate debt does not instantly reprice its coupons. The mechanism works through refinancing needs, floating-rate exposure, expected future funding and economic effects. Distinguish an increase in market yield from the effective interest rate on the whole debt stock.

Reference: [Sergeyev, Bocconi 30054, lecture 20, PDF pp. 17–22](https://drive.google.com/file/d/1CU1mi1ebsvbc5dh_VNRv7eoDWNOGfdwE/view?usp=drivesdk) [KOM, 12th Global Edition, ch. 21, printed pp. 722–725](https://drive.google.com/file/d/1-UaatD4D8-K1UynWK7AkRZ2fAZ3y04mI/view). ## Slide 25 — The bank–sovereign loop

This is an original mechanism diagram. The loop can begin with weak banks or with sovereign distress. Domestic sovereign concentration amplifies bank losses, while expected public guarantees transmit bank risk to the state. Accounting classification and hedges affect when losses appear, but economic exposure can remain.

Reference: [Sergeyev, Bocconi 30054, lecture 20, PDF pp. 18–22](https://drive.google.com/file/d/1CU1mi1ebsvbc5dh_VNRv7eoDWNOGfdwE/view?usp=drivesdk) [KOM, 12th Global Edition, ch. 21, printed pp. 722–725](https://drive.google.com/file/d/1-UaatD4D8-K1UynWK7AkRZ2fAZ3y04mI/view). ## Slide 26 — A historical backstop: OMT in 2012

Use the formal September 6 decision rather than equating the earlier July speech or August announcement with the final technical framework. OMT had a mandate, eligibility conditions and discretion; it was not unconditional fiscal insurance for every issuer. Discuss how credibility can change expected refinancing outcomes. No claim about its current deployment is needed.
