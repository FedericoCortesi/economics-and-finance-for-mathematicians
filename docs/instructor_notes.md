## Slide 14 — Equilibrium adds planned spending

The graph is the Keynesian cross: both axes measure real quantities/values at fixed prices, rather than the price and quantity of one product. The production/supply line records Y; planned demand here is the hypothetical relation 50+0.5Y. Solving Y=50+0.5Y gives Y=100. At Y=120, planned spending is only 110, so 10 accumulates as unintended inventory and firms may cut subsequent output. At Y=80, planned spending is 90, so inventories run down and firms may expand. This adjustment story needs price and behavioural assumptions. The ex post GDP identity still holds at every point because realised inventory investment reconciles production and expenditure. The crossing can lie below potential. Class 2 will derive the spending slope from household and investment behaviour.

## Slide 15 — Potential output and the long run

Cobb–Douglas is a specific causal/technological model of productive capacity, not an accounting identity. K is the capital stock used at normal utilisation, L* is labour at sustainable normal employment, and A captures productivity. The exponent alpha is the elasticity of output with respect to capital; 1−alpha is the labour elasticity. Holding A fixed, doubling both inputs doubles potential output. Each input separately has a diminishing marginal product. A factor-income share interpretation requires further competitive-market assumptions. More capital, labour or productivity shifts capacity. The long-run equilibrium benchmark closes the cyclical output gap, without assigning a fixed number of months to adjustment. Potential is an estimated sustainable benchmark, not a physical ceiling that output can never temporarily exceed.

Reference: [Sergeyev, Bocconi 30054, lecture 7, PDF pp. 15–17](https://drive.google.com/file/d/1K3Oi65qIJ45h_myuee0MRHowOwnMrEfb/view?usp=drivesdk) [Jahan and Mahmud, IMF, What Is the Output Gap?](https://www.imf.org/external/pubs/ft/fandd/2013/09/basics.htm). ## Slide 16 — The output gap connects activity to rates

The percentage gap compares actual output with potential output at the same date. It differs from GDP growth: output can grow while remaining below potential. A −5% gap means output is five percent below the sustainable benchmark, not that GDP fell five percent this year. Resource slack matters for hiring, wages, inflation pressure and the policy-rate response. These are conditional mechanisms: supply shocks, expectations and measurement uncertainty can change the relationship. Potential is unobserved and estimates are revised. A zero gap in the long-run benchmark does not mean zero GDP growth or zero inflation.

Reference: [Jahan and Mahmud, IMF, What Is the Output Gap?](https://www.imf.org/external/pubs/ft/fandd/2013/09/basics.htm). ## Slide 26 — The current account is our net current flow from abroad

The current account measures a net current flow between residents and nonresidents over a period. It is not the sum of every cross-border money transfer: financial-asset purchases belong in the financial account, and capital transfers have a separate capital account. In the prior hypothetical example, trade −12 plus primary income +4 and secondary income +3 gives CA=−5. With consistent gross definitions, CA=GNDI−C−G−I=S−I. A negative balance means the economy absorbs more goods/services than its disposable national income supports; the financing counterpart is net liability issuance or a reduction in foreign assets, allowing for KA and measurement discrepancies. This says why the funding question matters, not whether a deficit is desirable or which direction the currency must move.

## Slide 31 — Foreign investors help finance US spending

A current-account deficit means current external payments exceed receipts. A foreign purchase of a newly issued US bond is one way to supply financing. Foreign purchases of US shares or direct investments, or US residents selling foreign assets, can also contribute. The BEA observation is a quarterly flow, seasonally adjusted, not an annualised dollar amount and not the government fiscal deficit. Do not equate each import with an identifiable new Treasury bond. Actual financial-account data include capital-account entries, derivatives and statistical discrepancies. The simple no-error example remains CA=−10, KA=0, FA=−10 when new net liabilities finance the gap.

## Slide 32 — Net international investment position

Residents own foreign assets; nonresidents own claims on residents. The counterparty’s residence defines external, not the currency of denomination. A negative position describes a net external debtor in the broad financial sense, but its liabilities are not all fixed-repayment debt. This is the whole economy, not the Treasury balance sheet. Claims can be equity, direct investment, bonds, loans and deposits. Keep their gross size, maturity, currency, income and risk in view. The next slide puts actual US values on these definitions.

## Slide 33 — The US external balance sheet is large on both sides

These are BEA end-of-quarter stocks, in trillions of US dollars. The net position is the difference between two much larger gross positions. Negative NIIP is not a bill due tomorrow and is not identical to government debt. Its structure determines income and exposure. The numbers do not say that foreigners lend all these funds at the Treasury yield, because liabilities include US equities and direct investment. Values are the frozen 24 September 2026 release; later BEA revisions can change them. Optional reconciliation: ΔNIIP=CA+KA+valuation+other changes under consistent coverage and no discrepancy. Price and exchange-rate revaluations explain why cumulative CA alone cannot recover the current position. This detail is useful for data reading but is not the main teaching question here.

## Slide 34 — What is the US “exorbitant privilege”?

The classic phrase describes benefits from the dollar’s international role. Investors value liquidity and safety services as well as financial yield, so they may accept a lower yield on a dollar safe asset than on a comparable asset without those services. This is a relative funding advantage, not a claim that US nominal yields must be below every foreign yield: inflation, policy and risk differ. Gourinchas and Rey study the additional role of the external portfolio’s composition and historical return differentials. Higher risky returns are not guaranteed arbitrage. The stylised contrast is foreign demand for safe dollar claims and US ownership of risky businesses abroad; actual assets and liabilities both contain many instruments. The Fed 2025 note describes a historical baseline and is not treated as evidence that the privilege strengthened in 2026.

## Slide 35 — Negative NIIP can coexist with positive investment income

## Slide 2 — Short run and long run

This caveat comes before the lecture models. Short run and long run identify different adjustment assumptions, not universal durations. In the sticky-price short-run demand experiments, firms can vary production and policy can change real financing conditions when expected inflation does not adjust one-for-one. In the standard flexible-price long-run benchmark, output equals its current potential and real saving/investment fundamentals determine r*. A purely nominal shock then mainly changes nominal prices, inflation and nominal rates. That monetary-neutrality claim is a model benchmark, not a theorem about every persistent policy change. Productivity, capital, labour, preferences and fiscal institutions can also change, moving potential output and the real benchmark themselves. Shocks can leave lasting scars, adjustment need not be monotone, and market asset prices can react immediately to expected long-run effects. Never transfer a curve-shift result between horizons without checking assumptions. ## Slide 9 — A real return measures purchasing power

## Slide 10 — The Fisher approximation comes from logarithms
